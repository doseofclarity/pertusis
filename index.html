Communicable Disease Review

Pertussis
(Whooping Cough)

"The 100‑day cough – highly contagious, preventable by vaccine."

Epidemiology & Overview

Pertussis (whooping cough) – acute, highly communicable infection of the respiratory tract caused by Bordetella pertussis.

Public Health Significance

  • Predominantly a childhood disease, highest incidence under 7 years.
  • Mortality highest in infants under 6 months.
  • Severe complications: pneumonia, seizures, encephalopathy, death.
  • One attack confers definite and prolonged immunity (second attacks rare).

Agent & Reservoir

Etiologic agent: Bordetella pertussis (also called Hemophilus pertussis or Bordet-Gengou bacillus).

Source of infection: Discharges from laryngeal and bronchial mucous membranes of infected persons.

Infectivity: Extremely contagious, especially during the catarrhal stage.

Clinical Stages & Diagnosis

Three Clinical Stages

1. Catarrhal stage (1‑2 weeks): onset like common cold – coryza, sneezing, low‑grade fever, mild cough.
2. Paroxysmal stage (2‑6 weeks): paroxysms of rapid cough followed by inspiratory “whoop”, post‑tussive vomiting, cyanosis, exhaustion.
3. Convalescent stage (weeks to months): gradual decrease in cough severity; paroxysms may recur with subsequent respiratory infections.

Diagnosis

  • Clinical: Paroxysmal cough with whoop and post‑tussive vomiting.
  • Laboratory: Nasopharyngeal swab for PCR or culture (gold standard, but slow).
  • Serology: Anti‑pertussis toxin antibodies.
  • Blood count: Lymphocytosis (markedly elevated white blood cells with lymphocytosis).

Transmission & Communicability

Mode of Transmission

  • Direct spread: respiratory droplets (coughing, sneezing).
  • Close contact: crowding facilitates spread.
  • Incubation period: 7‑10 days (not exceeding 21 days).

Period of Communicability

  • Early catarrhal stage – most infectious (before paroxysmal cough).
  • From 7 days after exposure up to 3 weeks after onset of typical paroxysms.
  • Untreated patients remain infectious for about 3 weeks after cough onset.
  • With appropriate antibiotics (macrolides), communicability reduces to 5 days.

Booster doses: at 18‑24 months (DPT booster) and 4‑6 years (preschool booster).

Other Preventive Measures

  • Isolation of cases until 3 weeks after paroxysmal cough onset (or 5 days after starting antibiotics).
  • Post‑exposure prophylaxis (PEP) with macrolides for susceptible household contacts.
  • Cocooning strategy: vaccinate all close contacts of infants (parents, siblings, caregivers).

Treatment & Complications

Treatment

  • Antibiotics: Macrolides (azithromycin, clarithromycin, erythromycin) – shorten communicability, may reduce severity if given early in catarrhal stage.
  • Supportive care: oxygen, hydration, suction, monitoring for apnea.
  • Hospitalization: for infants <6 months, severe paroxysms, cyanosis, or complications.
  • Avoid cough suppressants – they do not help and may be harmful.

Complications

  • Pneumonia (most common cause of death).
  • Apnea, bradycardia (especially in infants).
  • Seizures, encephalopathy (due to hypoxia or toxin).
  • Otitis media, dehydration, weight loss.
  • Pneumothorax, hernia, subconjunctival hemorrhages (from forceful coughing).

PHN Role & Nursing Care

  • Health education: Emphasize dangers of pertussis, need for complete DPT immunization at 6,10,14 weeks.
  • Case finding & reporting: Pertussis is a notifiable disease (under NESSS clinically diagnosed).
  • Isolation: Advise families to keep child away from school/childcare for 3 weeks (or 5 days after starting antibiotics).
  • Nursing care during paroxysms:
    • Position upright, support abdominal muscles.
    • Provide cool, humidified oxygen if needed.
    • Small, frequent feeds to prevent vomiting.
    • Maintain airway; suction if needed.
    • Monitor for apnea and cyanosis.
  • Complication monitoring: watch for pneumonia, seizures, dehydration.
  • Contact tracing & prophylaxis: identify susceptible household contacts (especially infants) and offer antibiotics/vaccine.
